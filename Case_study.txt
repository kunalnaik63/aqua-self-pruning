The objective is a deliberate combination of ideas that does not exist as a ready-made recipe. To finish it you will have to derive things yourself, measure things yourself, and defend every decision in a live session where we will ask you to extend your own code in front of us.
You are welcome to use AI tools — we use them every day. But they will get you only part of the way here, and they will confidently get one specific thing wrong (we know which thing). We are hiring for deep technical depth. This challenge measures exactly that.
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── ABOUT AQUA ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
AQUA is our enterprise AI Agent Platform. We run agent inference at scale, which means we obsess over two things: the cost of every model we serve, and the correctness of the primitives no framework will write for you.
This challenge lives at that intersection. You will build a neural network that prunes itself while it trains, and you must prove it actually got cheaper — without overstating the result.
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── THE CHALLENGE: "THE SELF-PRUNING NETWORK" ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
You will build, from scratch in pure Python and NumPy, a small neural network library and use it to train a classifier that learns its own sparse structure during training — pruning away its least useful connections under a hard compute budget, while reasoning explicitly about the gradient consequences of doing so.
This is a real problem for us. On AQUA, a model that is four times smaller but 99% as accurate is often the difference between a feature shipping and not. The hard part is not "set small weights to zero." The hard part is doing it correctly: keeping your autodiff honest while the graph itself is changing underneath you, and proving the result is both sparse and accurate.
RESTRICTIONS (core implementation)
The following are not permitted for the core of your solution: PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX, Keras, HuggingFace, or any autodiff, pruning, or neural-network library. Scikit-learn may be used only to load a standard dataset — not for training, models, or gradients — and you must state this explicitly in your README.
The autodiff engine, backward pass, pruning mechanism, optimizer, and training loop must be your own work.
PART 1 — A REVERSE-MODE AUTODIFF ENGINE THAT SURVIVES A CHANGING GRAPH (Mandatory)
Build a minimal reverse-mode automatic differentiation engine with the following:
1.	A tensor type that wraps a NumPy array, tracks parents in a computation graph, and stores gradients.
2.	Differentiable operations, at minimum:
o	Element-wise addition, subtraction, multiplication, division
o	Matrix multiplication
o	Reductions: sum and mean
o	Non-linearities: ReLU, and at least one of tanh, sigmoid, or GELU
o	A numerically stable softmax combined with cross-entropy loss
3.	A backward() method with correct topological ordering and gradient accumulation, including the broadcasting case. For example, (N, D) + (D,) must backpropagate correctly.
4.	Gradient checking against finite-difference numerical gradients, with automated tests that pass for every operation you implemented.
CORRECTNESS REQUIREMENT (we will verify this specifically)
Your engine must remain correct when parts of the parameter tensors are masked out (frozen to zero) between training steps.
A masked weight must contribute zero to the forward pass and receive a well-defined gradient treatment. Your optimizer state (momentum, Adam moments) for a pruned-then-revived connection must not silently corrupt training.
Think carefully about what "the gradient of a masked weight" should be, and what your optimizer does with it. Most quick implementations get this subtly wrong. Write a test that demonstrates you got it right.
PART 2 — TRAIN A NETWORK WITH A REAL OPTIMIZER (Mandatory)
Using only your Part 1 engine:
1.	Implement a small MLP as composable layers (e.g. Linear + activation).
2.	Implement Adam from scratch. SGD with momentum is the minimum acceptable optimizer, but Adam is strongly preferred for Part 3.
3.	Train on a small classification task of your choice (e.g. MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, or a synthetic dataset such as spirals) using your own mini-batched training loop. Report a learning curve (loss and accuracy over epochs).
4.	Justify your weight initialization choices. Demonstrate stable training without NaNs.
PART 3 — MAKE IT PRUNE ITSELF (The core of the challenge)
Train the same network so that it progressively removes its own least-important connections during training and ends under a target sparsity, while staying accurate.
You must design and implement the following yourself:
1.	An importance criterion
Decide how a connection earns the right to survive. Magnitude-only pruning is the trivial baseline — we will ask why you can do better. Stronger answers use gradient or sensitivity information (for example, a saliency estimate such as |weight × gradient|, a Taylor expansion of the loss change from removing the connection, or a movement-based criterion). You must derive why your criterion approximates "loss increase if removed."
2.	A pruning schedule
Prune gradually under a schedule you justify (for example, a cubic sparsity ramp). One-shot pruning at the end of training is the weaker answer; explain the trade-off if you choose it.
3.	Honest masking
A pruned connection must be genuinely removed from the forward computation (a real zero, not a near-zero weight). Your autodiff and optimizer must handle the mask correctly (see the Part 1 correctness requirement). Bonus credit for allowing regrowth — a pruned connection returning if it becomes important again — and for reasoning about the stability implications.
4.	A hard budget
Hit a target sparsity (for example, 90% of connections removed) and report what it cost you in accuracy.
PART 4 — PROVE IT (Required evidence)
A pruned model that claims to be cheap but is not actually cheaper is worthless to us. You must produce evidence, not assertions:
1.	A sparsity–accuracy Pareto curve
Sweep target sparsity levels (for example: 0%, 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%). Plot final accuracy versus achieved sparsity. Commit both the plot and the raw numbers to your repository.
2.	A real cost measurement
Demonstrate that pruning actually reduced work — for example, measured FLOPs, active-parameter count, or wall-clock inference time on the sparse model using a sparse-aware forward path you wrote — not a dense forward pass that multiplies by zeros. If you use the dense-times-zero shortcut, say so explicitly and explain why it does not count as a real speedup. We would rather you be honest than overstate the result.
3.	A baseline comparison
Compare your importance-based pruning against pure magnitude pruning at the same sparsity. Is yours actually better? By how much? Is the difference real or noise? Show your reasoning about variance across random seeds.
4.	One falsifiable claim
State one specific, quantitative claim your results support (for example: "At 90% sparsity, saliency pruning retains X% accuracy versus Y% for magnitude pruning, averaged across N seeds") and back it with committed numbers in your repository.
